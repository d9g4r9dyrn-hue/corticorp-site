Feature

Thrust Buggy v2

An RC buggy that drives like a car and pushes like an airboat.

Version one was a dare: the twin ducted fans from a UMX A-10, strapped to a rear-wheel-drive buggy. One person drove, one ran the fans. It worked, it was clunky, and by its builder's estimate it passed 45 mph.

Version two makes it one machine. A single strong fan sits at the back and takes its orders from the car's own throttle. Rudder vanes behind the fan are linked to the steering: fixed, simple, no rotating nozzles to break. A 3D-printed body with fenders and an intake channel feeds the fan. First test: one 80mm fan on 6S.

More views of the concept. The styling here is exploratory.

The working drawing. This one is being built for real.

Feature

Squeeze lighter

Fire from a handshake.

Every lighter runs out. This one cannot, because there is nothing in it to run out of. Squeeze the lever and a gear train turns one squeeze into many turns of a small generator. The current either heats a coil until it glows or jumps a gap as an arc.

It is an emergency lighter: the kind that waits in a drawer or a pack and works on the first squeeze. The idea has been carried around for more than 25 years. A flywheel version, which would bank a few squeezes and spend them at once, is set aside for now.

Feature

Gyroscope cantilever

A beam that should fall, and doesn't.

Hold a beam at one end only and the other end drops. Put a heavy spinning wheel on the free end and it stops dropping: the beam hangs level and slowly circles its post.

It is an old physics demonstration and it still looks like a trick. A desk-sized build for two people, one of them young.

Now scale it up. The idea came from a famous house whose terraces hang out over a waterfall, held there by the mass of the building behind them. A cantilever normally needs that counterweight. A gyroscope does not: stand the wheel upright on the free end, its shaft in line with the arm, and a terrace, a deck or a whole bridge can reach out over open air from a single support. Make the arm long enough to cross the gorge and you have a bridge with nothing under it.

There are two catches. The wheel must never stop, so assume a power source that never fails. And a gyroscope holds a load up by turning, so the whole arm creeps slowly around its support. Treat that as the feature: a terrace that sweeps the gorge, a deck that shows you the whole canyon by lunchtime.

The house. One terrace, one support, and a motor-driven flywheel at the tip, where no counterweight could go.
